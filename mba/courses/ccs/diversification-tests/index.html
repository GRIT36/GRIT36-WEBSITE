Session 3 / Growth

Three tests for diversification

Before increasing horizontal, vertical or geographical scope, ask whether the chosen area is attractive, whether entry costs leave room for returns, and whether combining the businesses creates an advantage beyond stand-alone operation.

ENGLISH STUDY GUIDE Edited explanation based on the course. Original wording and figures appear below.

The framework

TestCore questionWhat an answer must establish
AttractivenessIs the target area structurally attractive, or can it be made attractive?A reasoned view of competitive structure rather than growth alone.
Cost of entryWill the full cost of entering absorb the future benefits?Acquisition or start-up costs plus the resources needed to develop, adapt and integrate.
Better-off / added valueDoes the new business gain competitive advantage from the corporation, or vice versa?An identifiable mechanism producing value beyond the businesses' independent performance.

Key distinctions

Possible mechanisms behind the better-off test

The course discusses operating and investment cost synergies, revenue synergies and financial synergies. In vertical settings, relationship-specific investments, double marginalization and free-riding problems can also matter. The relevant mechanism depends on the relationship between the businesses.

Six questions before diversifying

The lecture also asks what the firm does better than competitors, what strategic assets the new market requires, whether it can catch up or leapfrog, whether entry would break apart assets that need to stay together, whether it can become a winner rather than merely a participant, and what it can learn from the move. Organizational ability to learn is part of the assessment.

How to use it

  1. Separate evidence for each test instead of treating one strong argument as sufficient.
  2. Explain how the proposed link creates value and who must act to realize it.
  3. Compare the proposed scope with feasible alternatives, including remaining independent.

Keep in mind

Attractive demand does not establish a better-off effect. A vague claim of synergy also does not account for coordination and ownership costs.

Pause & recall

Why can an attractive acquisition still fail the tests?

Reveal the explanation

Its entry cost can consume the gains, or ownership can fail to create additional competitive advantage.

S03 / Corporate scope

The three dimensions of corporate scope

Corporate scope describes the perimeter of the company as a whole: the businesses it participates in, the stages of the value chain it undertakes and the geographical markets it serves. Corporate strategy determines this scope and how the activities are coordinated to create value.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Corporate scope

Types of diversification & relatedness

The course classifies diversification by how the new business relates to existing activities, resources, skills, products and customers. The central question is which links can actually be shared or transferred, rather than whether two businesses sound similar.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Growth

The Ansoff growth matrix

The Ansoff matrix organizes growth alternatives by products and markets. It distinguishes deeper participation in the current product–market combination from developing a new market, developing a new product or changing both.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Internationalization

CAGE & international market entry

Internationalization can describe both the extent of foreign-market participation and a strategy of expansion across borders. Country choice and entry mode should be assessed together because differences affect opportunities, capabilities, costs and control.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Growth and exit

Growth modes, reorganization & divestiture

A direction of growth is different from the means used to achieve it. The course compares organic development, network-based growth and acquired growth, and treats reorganization and divestiture as part of managing corporate scope.
