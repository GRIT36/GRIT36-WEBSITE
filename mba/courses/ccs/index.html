Competitive & Corporate Strategy

Why do firms outperform one another? Which businesses belong together? How can an organization renew itself? Six sessions connect these questions, from competitive advantage to strategic renewal.

42 frameworks & concepts
S01 / Foundations

The strategic management process

Strategy links long-term direction, choices and resource allocation. The course treats it as a connected process: strategic inputs inform formulation and implementation; outcomes and experience feed back into the next round of decisions.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Foundations

Industry position & resource-based advantage

The I/O model and the resource-based model explain above-average returns from different starting points. One begins with industry structure; the other begins with the firm's resources and capabilities. They provide complementary questions for diagnosing an opportunity.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Strategy formation

Six schools of strategy

The course distinguishes prescriptive schools, which explain how strategy should be formulated, from descriptive schools, which examine how strategy actually forms. Each emphasizes different actors, assumptions and sources of insight.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Strategy formation

Deliberate & emergent strategy

Realized strategy can contain both deliberate choices and patterns that emerge through action. The lecture separates what was intended from what was implemented, abandoned or learned along the way.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Industry analysis

Porter's Five Forces

Five Forces examines how competition over economic value extends beyond direct rivals. The focal unit is an industry: suppliers, buyers, substitutes and potential entrants can all constrain its profit potential.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Positioning

Generic competitive strategies

Generic strategies combine the source of advantage with the breadth of the competitive target. The central choices are cost leadership, differentiation and focus; the slides also discuss integrated or hybrid positioning.

Read the framework ↗S01 / Positioning

Bowman's Strategy Clock

The Strategy Clock maps competitive positions using price and perceived value to the customer. It makes room for combinations such as low price with low added value, low price, hybrid value, differentiation and focused differentiation.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Competitive change

Hypercompetition & invisible S-curves

A business model can lose strategic strength before its financial results deteriorate. The course contrasts relatively stable competition with hypercompetition, where advantages are shorter-lived and renewal must occur more frequently.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model

The Business Model Canvas

A business model describes the connected activities through which an organization meets a market need and captures value. The nine-block canvas makes that logic visible: customer choices, delivery arrangements and financial consequences should support one another.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model

Four digital business model types

The course's digital business model matrix combines two dimensions: knowledge of the end customer, from partial to complete, and business design, from a value chain to an ecosystem. The resulting four positions imply different relationships and capabilities.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model innovation

Replication, renewal & dual focus

Business model replication develops and extends an existing logic; business model renewal changes the model more substantially. The lecture distinguishes fixation, a focus on one route and dual focus on both routes.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model innovation

Business model transformation routes

The innovation matrix combines replication versus renewal with strategy-driven versus customer-driven change. Its four routes link the direction of change to leadership, managerial involvement, knowledge absorption and organizational identity.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model innovation

Four levers of business model innovation

Technology can enable new activities, but it is only one of the course's four levers. Management practices, organizational forms and co-creation affect whether an innovation can actually change how value is created and captured.

Read the framework ↗S02 / Business model innovation

Enablers & inhibitors of innovation

The same transformation proposal can progress differently in different organizations. The course groups enabling and inhibiting conditions around culture, management, institutions, external orientation and organizational characteristics.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Corporate scope

The three dimensions of corporate scope

Corporate scope describes the perimeter of the company as a whole: the businesses it participates in, the stages of the value chain it undertakes and the geographical markets it serves. Corporate strategy determines this scope and how the activities are coordinated to create value.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Corporate scope

Types of diversification & relatedness

The course classifies diversification by how the new business relates to existing activities, resources, skills, products and customers. The central question is which links can actually be shared or transferred, rather than whether two businesses sound similar.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Growth

The Ansoff growth matrix

The Ansoff matrix organizes growth alternatives by products and markets. It distinguishes deeper participation in the current product–market combination from developing a new market, developing a new product or changing both.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Growth

Three tests for diversification

Before increasing horizontal, vertical or geographical scope, ask whether the chosen area is attractive, whether entry costs leave room for returns, and whether combining the businesses creates an advantage beyond stand-alone operation.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Internationalization

CAGE & international market entry

Internationalization can describe both the extent of foreign-market participation and a strategy of expansion across borders. Country choice and entry mode should be assessed together because differences affect opportunities, capabilities, costs and control.

Read the framework ↗S03 / Growth and exit

Growth modes, reorganization & divestiture

A direction of growth is different from the means used to achieve it. The course compares organic development, network-based growth and acquired growth, and treats reorganization and divestiture as part of managing corporate scope.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Corporate value

Synergy & its hidden costs

Synergy exists when businesses achieve greater value together than they could independently. The course distinguishes the mechanisms creating that gain and the coordination costs, reduced responsiveness and managerial biases that can offset it.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Corporate value

Corporate parenting roles

A corporate parent is the organizational layer above the business units, not necessarily only the central headquarters. It must explain how its involvement improves the businesses rather than simply increasing administration.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Portfolio analysis

Measuring diversification

The Wrigley/Rumelt classification uses revenue ratios to distinguish single, dominant, related and unrelated businesses. The lecture then separates related-constrained and related-linked portfolios by the strength of a common core.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Portfolio analysis

The BCG growth–share matrix

The BCG matrix compares relative market share with market growth. It provides a compact way to discuss the competitive position, cash generation and resource needs of businesses within a portfolio.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Portfolio analysis

Portfolio matrices beyond BCG

Different matrices address different allocation questions. The course compares market growth and share with competitive position, life-cycle stage, market attractiveness and the corporate parent's ability to add value.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Organization

Strategy–structure alignment

The multidivisional form consists of operating divisions with their own functional hierarchies. The course compares cooperative, strategic-business-unit and competitive variants, showing how coordination should reflect the relatedness of businesses.

Read the framework ↗S04 / Internationalization

International strategy & organization

International strategy involves a tension between global integration and local responsiveness. The course connects multidomestic, global and combined strategies to different arrangements of geographical and product responsibilities.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Internal analysis

Value chain & activity systems

Value-chain analysis identifies the activities through which an organization transforms inputs into outputs. The activity-system view then asks how resources and capabilities connect across those activities to support a strategic position.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Internal analysis

The VRINO framework

VRINO examines whether a resource, routine, capability or competence can support sustained competitive advantage. The course uses five tests: Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable and Organized.

Read the framework ↗S05 / External analysis

PESTEL macro-environmental analysis

PESTEL organizes changes in the wider environment into six categories. Its purpose in the course's analysis sequence is to identify relevant external developments before examining industry structure, competitors and customers.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Industry analysis

Industry structure, life cycle & strategic groups

The course separates three levels of industry analysis: industry characteristics, strategic groups and rivalry between specific competitors. These levels answer different questions and should not be collapsed into one competitor list.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Industry analysis

The four corners of competitor analysis

To reason about a competitor's future strategy, examine its drivers, management assumptions, current strategy and capabilities. The course places these four perspectives around the question of what the competitor is likely to do next.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Strategic diagnosis

SWOT as a synthesis of analysis

In the course sequence, SWOT is the result of internal and external analysis. Strengths and weaknesses concern the organization; opportunities and threats concern the environment. The useful work lies in explaining how these factors interact.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Ecosystems

Ecosystems, complements & structure

An ecosystem links multiple actors through complementary contributions to a value proposition without placing them all under one hierarchy. The analysis asks how joint value comes about and which interdependencies must be aligned.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Alliances

Alliance forms & governance

Alliances coordinate activities across firm boundaries. The lecture distinguishes equity-based arrangements from contractual arrangements, alongside other associational forms, and explains how the form affects commitment and coordination.

Read the framework ↗S05 / Alliances

Partner selection, coopetition & alliance dynamics

The choice of a partner is only the beginning. Alliance performance depends on the fit of contributions and the continuing management of interdependence, uncertainty and changing objectives after formation.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Renewal foundations

Strategic renewal: exploration & exploitation

Strategic renewal changes the organization’s existing path: its competences, assumptions, activities and relationship with the environment. The lecture contrasts selection pressures with managerial adaptation, and exploitation of existing strengths with exploration of new possibilities.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Environmental fit

Organizational flexibility

Flexibility is a response to environmental demands, supported by management capabilities and organizational design. The lecture distinguishes rigid, planned, flexible and chaotic forms. More change is not automatically better: the required flexibility must be compared with the organization’s actual and potential flexibility.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Changing the organization

Renewal paths & the transition dip

Renewal is a transition between organizational forms, not simply a decision to become more flexible. The lecture contrasts routinization with revitalization and illustrates the possibility of a temporary performance decline during transformation.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Balancing competing demands

Organizational ambidexterity

Ambidexterity is the capacity to pursue exploration and exploitation together. The lecture compares structural separation with a context that lets individuals balance both, and introduces four ways of combining different organizational forms.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Roles & integration

Four journeys of strategic renewal

The lecture distinguishes renewal journeys by the exploration or exploitation orientation of top management and of middle/front-line management. Each journey assigns a different role to initiative, integration and industry rules.

Read the framework ↗S06 / Leading renewal

Transactional & transformational leadership

The lecture contrasts leadership based on exchanges, coordination and established expectations with leadership that mobilizes people around changed values, aspirations and direction. The comparison provides a starting point for considering how leadership supports strategic renewal.
